Self-Study Curriculum · Programming Languages & Type Theory

A Program
in PL Theory & Implementation

Intended for a working engineer with a CS degree, functional programming exposure, and serious intent. I tried calibrate for someone (like myself) returning to academic mathematics after time away.

I have not gone to graduate school, and I don't know if I will ever have time to do that. But I really like studying programming languages and, it turns out, type theory. So I'm trying to study it anyways. I've built up a small reading list over time, but this is my attempt, with some assistance from Claude, to organize it into a curriculum. If you happen to be a PhD in this field, and you have thoughts or feedback about the curriculum that Claude and I have generated here, I'd love to hear more, please feel free to email me.

Six phases, roughly sequential, though some topics may cross phase boundaries occasionally. Phase I addresses mathematical proof-writing; I found when I started to read Winskel that I lacked background in this, and it felt significant enough that I wanted to cover it well. Phases II–IV cover the classical core of PL theory. Phases V–VI cover more advanced material and possibly even research areas. Again, I am not an expert in any of this; these books and papers have been gathered mostly as I've followed my own interests, and noted the books and papers that are cited by whatever I'm reading at the time, and some of them were suggested by Claude as being relevant to their particular section.

Each phase contains required books, papers to read, and implementation projects. I've no idea how long this program would take to complete; a few years at least, I expect, and it probably depends on how much time you have or care to spend on it. Will I even finish it? Great question, but I do want to try. This curriculum was generated with a strong bias toward my current interests. I know there are gaps here; I will most likely be amending and adding (or removing) things as I go on.

Please note: I fed Claude a lot of books I was interested in, topics I was interested in, and asked it to organize it into a self-study program, with an intentional ordering, and including suggestions for projects and exercises. I've rewritten some of the text on this page, and I'll probably rewrite more. So all the book & paper descriptions that I haven't got to, well, those are described by Claude. Since most of them are books and papers that I haven't read yet, I don't know that I can offer up anything different to say in the way of descriptions, yet. I plan to update the book notes as I get through the content.

I
Phase One
Mathematical Foundations & Proof
The proof-writing reflex atrophies without use. Or, in my case, I don't think my undergrad education covered proofs at all. From what I have seen so far, everything downstream — induction over terms, subject reduction proofs, normalization arguments — requires fluency here. If you did have this sort of material in your undergrad program, you might skip over or lightly review this phase.
Books
  • How to Prove It — Daniel Velleman core
    Seems like the right starting point: logic, sets, relations, functions, mathematical induction. Do the exercises. The chapters on relations and functions seem important.
  • Naive Set Theory — Paul Halmos math
    Spare and beautiful, and surprisingly funny (at least, I thought so). I discovered Halmos from seeing it referenced from Winskel. Maybe read alongside Velleman rather than after. Ordinals and cardinals appear later in domain theory and denotational semantics; better to meet them here first.
  • Book of Proof — Richard Hammack math
    Free PDF. Looks like a good supplement to Velleman. Since there is likely a lot of overlap, use selectively rather than cover-to-cover.
  • Logic in Computer Science — Huth & Ryan core
    Propositional and predicate logic, natural deduction, temporal logic, model checking. This is one that Claude suggested, I don't know much of it's contents yet.
  • Concrete Mathematics — Graham, Knuth & Patashnik math
    I've been aware of this book for years, but have never read it. Claude suggests it will be valuable for combinatorial reasoning and summation manipulation. Seems reasonable.
Papers & Essays
  • "Notation as a Tool of Thought" — Kenneth Iverson, 1979 Turing Lecture paper
  • "Why Functional Programming Matters" — John Hughes, 1990 paper
Exercises
  • Complete all exercises in Velleman Chapters 1–6; do not skip the relation and function chapters
  • Work through the natural deduction proof trees in Huth & Ryan Chapter 1 by hand, on paper
  • Write informal proofs of basic set-theoretic identities (De Morgan, distributivity) in your own words before consulting any reference
II
Phase Two
Lambda Calculus & Functional Programming Foundations
The untyped lambda calculus is the atom from which PL theory is built. This phase establishes it rigorously and develops the Haskell fluency needed for everything that follows. I'm also not opposed to developing some OCaml fluency, so when I get here I may well augment this with some of that.
Books
  • An Introduction to Functional Programming through Lambda Calculus — Greg Michaelson core
    Underrated. Builds lambda calculus from scratch with an implementation thread throughout. An ideal bridge between prior Scheme exposure and formal theory.
  • Programming in Haskell — Graham Hutton core
    The right way to learn Haskell for someone doing PL theory. Concise, mathematically honest. Complete it before moving to Phase III.
  • The Haskell Road to Logic, Maths and Programming — Doets & van Eijck math
    Covers Phase I topics through Haskell — sets, relations, induction, number theory. A valuable second pass once Hutton is done; reinforces both mathematical and Haskell fluency simultaneously.
  • Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs — Abelson & Sussman impl
    If prior Scheme exposure was shallow, SICP deepens it. Chapters 4–5 (metacircular evaluator, register machine) are directly relevant to interpreter implementation.
  • The Lambda Calculus: Its Syntax and Semantics — H.P. Barendregt advanced
    The definitive reference. Not a cover-to-cover read at this stage — use it for Church-Rosser, normal forms, and combinatory logic. Return repeatedly throughout the program.
Papers
  • "Recursive Functions of Symbolic Expressions and Their Computation by Machine" — John McCarthy, 1960 paper
  • "Can Programming Be Liberated from the Von Neumann Style?" — John Backus, 1978 Turing Lecture paper
  • "The Mechanical Evaluation of Expressions" — Peter Landin, 1964 paper
  • "The Next 700 Programming Languages" — Peter Landin, 1966 paper
Projects
  • Implement an untyped lambda calculus interpreter with β-reduction and normal-order evaluation
  • Add Church encoding of booleans, naturals, and pairs; verify they compute correctly
  • Implement SKI combinator translation and verify that translated terms reduce to the same normal forms
  • Complete Hutton's exercises throughout; implement his expression evaluator and parser from scratch
  • Work through SICP Chapter 4 (metacircular evaluator) selectively
III
Phase Three
Type Theory: The Classical Core
TAPL is the central text of this phase and of the entire curriculum. Simple types, subtyping, System F, references, exceptions, recursive types. This phase takes as long as it takes.
Books
  • Types and Programming Languages — Benjamin Pierce core
    The central text of the curriculum. Read in full; work the exercises; implement the systems. The OCaml implementations in the book's artifact are worth running and modifying. Rewards multiple readings.
  • Practical Foundations for Programming Languages — Robert Harper core
    Free PDF from CMU. Harper's approach is distinct from Pierce's and essential as a second voice. His treatment of statics and dynamics is unusually clear; the abstract binding tree framework is worth knowing.
  • Advanced Topics in Types and Programming Languages — Pierce (ed.) core
    Edited volume covering effect types, dependent types, substructural types, and more. Read selectively alongside Phases IV–V; not a linear read. The Walker chapter on substructural types is essential.
Papers
  • "A Formulation of the Simple Theory of Types" — Alonzo Church, 1940 paper
  • "The Principal Type-Scheme of an Object in Combinatory Logic" — Hindley, 1969 paper
  • "A Theory of Type Polymorphism in Programming" — Robin Milner, 1978 paper
  • "Principal Type-Schemes for Functional Programs" — Damas & Milner, 1982 paper
  • "Towards a Theory of Type Structure" — John Reynolds, 1974 paper
  • "Types, Abstraction and Parametric Polymorphism" — John Reynolds, 1983 — parametricity paper
  • "Theorems for Free!" — Philip Wadler, 1989 paper
  • "How to Make ad-hoc Polymorphism Less ad-hoc" — Wadler & Blott, 1989 paper
  • "The Essence of Functional Programming" — Wadler, 1992 — introduces monads to FP paper
  • "Monads for Functional Programming" — Wadler, 1995 paper
Projects
  • Implement simply typed lambda calculus with type checking and small-step operational semantics
  • Extend with product types, sum types, and unit; write out progress and preservation proofs informally
  • Implement Algorithm W (Hindley-Milner type inference) from scratch — this is a milestone
  • Implement a small language with let-polymorphism; test with examples requiring generalization
  • Implement System F with Church-style type annotations
  • Implement one of the TAPL artifact systems (fullsimple, fullpoly, or fullref) and extend it with a feature of your own
gap   Formal Operational Semantics & Structural Proof Methods

Many self-study programs in type theory underemphasize the mechanics of formal operational semantics and the structural proof style used in PL papers. Progress and preservation, strong normalization, confluence — these are not just topics to read about; they are proof techniques to practice.

The Formal Semantics of Programming Languages (Winskel) covers this, and belongs here as a companion to TAPL. Its exercises on relations and structural induction are exactly the ones that feel hard until they don't. This book also introduces denotational semantics and domain theory, making it a bridge to Phase V.

Semantics of Programming Languages (Gunter) is complementary to Winskel with stronger domain theory coverage. Optional but valuable for someone who wants that depth earlier.

IV
Phase Four
Proof Theory, Curry-Howard, & Dependent Types
The Curry-Howard correspondence is the intellectual center of gravity of the entire field. This phase builds toward it from the proof theory side and introduces dependent types as its natural extension.
Books
  • Programming Language Foundations in Agda — Philip Wadler et al. core
    Free online. Teaches TAPL-style PL theory through dependent type proofs in Agda. Covers Curry-Howard directly. The PL framing makes it the right place to encounter dependent types for someone coming from TAPL.
  • Lectures on the Curry-Howard Isomorphism — Sørensen & Urzyczyn core
    The book dedicated entirely to this topic. Goes from simple types through System F through second-order logic. Technically demanding; uniquely focused.
  • Basic Proof Theory — Troelstra & Schwichtenberg math
    Natural deduction, sequent calculus, cut elimination (Gentzen's Hauptsatz), normalization. This is where Gentzen's work lives in textbook form. Dense; work through it slowly.
  • Type Theory and Formal Proof: An Introduction — Nederpelt & Geuvers math
    Covers the Lambda Cube (Barendregt's classification of type systems) systematically. Good on the pure type systems framework. More accessible than Barendregt's full treatment.
  • Proofs and Fundamentals — Ethan Bloch math
    If proof fluency is still unsteady after Phase I, this is the most thorough remediation available. Particularly strong on mathematical induction and its structural variants.
Papers
  • "Propositions as Types" — Philip Wadler, 2015 — the essential historical survey paper
  • "The formulae-as-types notion of construction" — William Howard, circulated 1969, published 1980 paper
  • "An intuitionistic theory of types" — Per Martin-Löf, 1975 paper
  • "Untersuchungen über das logische Schließen" — Gerhard Gentzen, 1935 — read in translation paper
  • "On formally undecidable propositions of Principia Mathematica" — Gödel, 1931 — read a good translation with commentary paper
  • "A tutorial implementation of a dependently typed lambda calculus" — Löh, McBride & Swierstra, 2010 paper
  • "Bidirectional Typing" — Dunfield & Krishnaswami, 2021 — survey of bidirectional type checking paper
Projects
  • Work through PLFA Parts I and II in full, completing all exercises in Agda
  • Implement a bidirectional type checker for STLC — this is the practical technique underlying real type checkers
  • Implement LambdaPi (from Löh, McBride, Swierstra) — a minimal dependently typed language
  • Prove progress and preservation for STLC in Agda
  • Formalize at least one result from earlier phases in Agda — e.g., commutativity of addition on Peano naturals, or a property of your earlier lambda calculus implementation
V
Phase Five
Semantics, Domains, & Category Theory
Denotational semantics, domain theory, and category theory give a mathematical language for talking about meaning rather than reduction. Scott, Plotkin, Moggi, and Reynolds live here.
Books
  • The Formal Semantics of Programming Languages — Glynn Winskel core
    Operational, denotational, and axiomatic semantics. Domain theory, fixed points, full abstraction. The exercises are genuinely hard and genuinely worth doing. This is a major text.
  • Category Theory — Steve Awodey math
    More accessible than Mac Lane; covers similar ground with more attention to the type-theoretic and logical connections. The right first read into category theory for someone approaching from PL.
  • Categories for the Working Mathematician — Saunders Mac Lane math
    The canonical reference. Functors, natural transformations, adjunctions, monads in the categorical sense. Chapters 1–4 carefully; the rest selectively. Everyone returns to this eventually.
  • Category Theory for Programmers — Bartosz Milewski impl
    Free online. The most accessible entry to category theory for working programmers. Read before or alongside Awodey as a motivating companion.
  • Practical Foundations of Mathematics — Paul Taylor advanced
    Unusual and brilliant — unifies set theory, type theory, logic, and category theory. Dense; use as a reference and perspective-giver rather than a linear read.
Papers
  • "LCF Considered as a Programming Language" — Gordon Plotkin, 1977 paper
  • "Notions of Computation and Monads" — Eugenio Moggi, 1991 — the categorical foundation of monads paper
  • "Computational Lambda-Calculus and Monads" — Moggi, 1989 paper
  • "Data Types à la Carte" — Wouter Swierstra, 2008 paper
  • "An Introduction to Algebraic Effects and Handlers" — Matija Pretnar, 2015 paper
  • "Continuations and the Nature of Universal CPS Transformations" — Plotkin; see also Thielecke's survey paper
Projects
  • Implement denotational semantics for the while-language from Winskel and verify equivalence with the operational semantics
  • Implement a continuation-passing style (CPS) transformer for lambda calculus
  • Implement algebraic effects and handlers in a small language
  • Work through Awodey Chapters 1–5 with pencil: verify the commutative diagrams by hand
  • Implement a small type-and-effect system (e.g., tracking IO or state effects in a toy language)
gap   Substructural & Linear Type Theory

Linear types and substructural logics are a commonly omitted thread that connects proof theory to resource management, concurrency, and systems programming. Rust's ownership model is a substructural type system; session types are built on linear logic.

"Linear logic" — Jean-Yves Girard, 1987 — is the founding paper. Technically demanding but worth reading for the structural rules discussion alone (contraction, weakening, exchange — and their absence).

ATTAPL Chapter 1 (Substructural Type Systems, Walker) covers this accessibly. "Propositions as Sessions" (Wadler, 2012) connects linear logic to session types for concurrent communication — a beautiful paper.

VI
Phase Six
Advanced Type Theory & Research Frontier
Homotopy type theory, cubical type theory, and the frontier of proof assistants. This phase is open-ended by design; it points toward active research rather than a fixed curriculum.
Books
  • Homotopy Type Theory — Univalent Foundations Program core
    Free online. The univalence axiom, higher inductive types, and the connection between type theory and homotopy theory. Chapters 1–4 are essential; beyond that depends on your direction. Plant the seed in Phase IV; return here fully.
  • Software Foundations (all volumes) — Pierce et al. impl
    Free online. Volume 1 (Logical Foundations) covers Coq. Volume 2 (PL Foundations) reprises TAPL content inside Coq. An alternative or complement to PLFA if Coq is preferred over Agda.
  • Certified Programming with Dependent Types — Adam Chlipala advanced
    Free online. Coq, proof automation, and certified programs. Takes dependent types seriously as a programming tool. Heavy; use selectively and in parallel with other texts.
  • Dependent Types at Work — Bove & Dybjer impl
    A short, practical tutorial on dependent type programming in Agda. Good for consolidating Phase IV material before proceeding further.
Papers
  • "Univalent Foundations of Mathematics" — Vladimir Voevodsky, 2011 paper
  • "Cubical Type Theory: a constructive interpretation of the univalence axiom" — Cohen, Coquand, Huber & Mörtberg, 2016 paper
  • "Why Dependent Types Matter" — Altenkirch, McBride & McKinna, 2005 paper
  • "The View from the Left" — McBride & McKinna, 2004 — dependent pattern matching paper
  • "Outrageous but Meaningful Coincidences" — Conor McBride, 2010 paper
  • "Syntax and Semantics of Dependent Types" — Martin Hofmann, 1997 paper
  • "Sterling and Harper: Logical Relations as Types" — 2021 — on synthetic Tait computability paper
Projects
  • Formalize a non-trivial mathematical result in Agda or Coq — e.g., Cantor's theorem, or decidability of a small type system
  • Implement a type checker for a small dependently typed language with Π and Σ types, with elaboration
  • Work through HoTT Book Chapter 2 (homotopy structure of types) with companions from the Agda HoTT library
  • Contribute a small formalization to an existing Agda or Coq library
  • Read one paper from a recent POPL, ICFP, or LICS proceedings in full and write a summary of its contributions
gap   History, Philosophy & Intellectual Context

A curriculum in PL theory without historical context produces technical competence without understanding of why the field looks the way it does. The following are not supplementary — they are part of the program.

Gödel, Escher, Bach (Hofstadter) — belongs at the beginning. Its treatment of self-reference, formal systems, and incompleteness is both accessible and genuinely deep.

"On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem" (Turing, 1936) and "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (Turing, 1950) — short papers that should be read in full. The 1936 paper is the founding document of computation.

From Frege to Gödel (van Heijenoort, ed.) — primary source anthology including Frege, Russell, Hilbert, Herbrand, and Gödel in translation. Invaluable for understanding the historical context of mathematical logic that PL theory inherits.

The Art of the Metaobject Protocol (Kiczales, des Rivières, Bobrow) — for someone from a Lisp/Ruby background, one of the deepest explorations of language extensibility and reflective systems ever written.

This curriculum covers approximately the content of a two-to-three year graduate program in programming languages, across six phases. The mathematics in Phases I, IV, and V is genuinely hard; plan for periods where progress is slow. The paper list is substantial but most papers are 20–40 pages; reading one paper per week throughout the program covers it comfortably.

Several books appear across phases deliberately: TAPL and ATTAPL reward re-reading as background accumulates. The same is true of Barendregt. The projects are where the material consolidates — reading without implementation leaves the understanding incomplete.
